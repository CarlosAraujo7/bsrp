\title{Evolutionary, Learning and Planning Methods for Dynamic Bike Repositioning: A Controlled Comparison}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Authors}}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Dynamic bike repositioning is a stochastic sequential decision problem usually attacked with reinforcement learning (RL) or lookahead planning; evolutionary computation
(EC) offers alternatives that are rarely compared with them under one protocol. On Citi Bike instances with 4 to 30 stations and 24 real days, we compare calibrated
heuristics, a DQN, a dual-policy DQN, a Monte Carlo rollout and three EC methods: CMA-ES calibration of the heuristics, OpenAI-ES neuroevolution of the DQN's network and
a rolling horizon evolutionary algorithm (RHEA). Training seeds are the unit of analysis, hyperparameters are chosen on validation only, budgets are counted in simulator
steps, and every number is generated from run logs. CMA-ES matches grid-calibrated heuristics (differences below 1\%). Neuroevolution is the weakest method at every scale:
even with 64$\times$ the DQN's simulator steps it never reaches the DQN and, within the noise of 3 seeds, only approaches the heuristic. A larger RHEA beats the
500k-step DQN at every scale and the rollout at 4 stations, but falls behind the rollout as stations grow. Adding the frozen DQN's value at the end of each evolved plan
makes RHEA match the DQN at small scales and, with a larger search, beat the DQN it bootstraps from at 12 and 30 stations and tie the rollout at 12. The DQN cuts unmet demand by \cecRDqnA\% at 4 stations but
only \cecRDqnE\% at 30, and planners that query the demand model overtake it from 12 stations on. We report negative results and the study's limits.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Station-based bike-sharing systems drift out of balance: empty stations cannot serve rentals and full stations cannot accept returns. Operators move bikes with
vehicles, and the \emph{dynamic} version of the problem---deciding, as demand unfolds, where the vehicle goes next and how many bikes it loads or unloads---is a
stochastic sequential decision problem. The literature attacks it with approximate dynamic programming, lookahead policies and, more recently, reinforcement learning
(RL)~\cite{seo2022,liang2024,brinkmann2019,guo2024}. Evolutionary computation (EC) offers three natural alternatives that are rarely compared against these
methods under a single protocol: evolutionary \emph{policy search} (neuroevolution)~\cite{salimans2017,such2017,bai2023}, evolutionary \emph{calibration} of
parametric heuristics~\cite{hansen2001}, and evolutionary \emph{online planning} with a model (rolling horizon evolution)~\cite{perezliebana2013}.

This paper compares them in a controlled way. On instances calibrated with real Citi Bike trips (4--30 stations, plus 24 real test days), we evaluate (i) greedy and
threshold heuristics calibrated by grid search and by CMA-ES; (ii) value-based RL (DQN) and a dual-policy variant adapted from DPRL~\cite{liang2024}; (iii) neuroevolution
with an evolution strategy (ES) on the same network as the DQN; and (iv) model-based planners, a Monte Carlo rollout and a rolling horizon evolutionary algorithm (RHEA).
All learners share one protocol: the unit of analysis is the training seed, hyperparameters are chosen on validation only, the test set is touched once, and every
budget is counted in simulator steps~\cite{henderson2018,agarwal2021}. Our contributions are: (a)~the comparison itself, including negative results;
(b)~evidence on how each family scales from 4 to 30 stations and on its dependence on the training budget; (c)~a test of three natural explanations for the decline of the
RL advantage with scale (network capacity, training budget and action-space size); (d)~a simple evolution--learning hybrid, RHEA with the value of a frozen DQN, and where it helps; and (e)~a verified, reproducible protocol with all numbers generated from
run logs.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Static and dynamic repositioning.} Operations research treats overnight repositioning as a pickup-and-delivery routing problem with complexity
results~\cite{benchimol2011}, mixed-integer formulations~\cite{raviv2013,dellamico2014} and cluster-first heuristics~\cite{schuijbroek2017}. For the dynamic case,
Ghosh \emph{et al.}~\cite{ghosh2017} optimize routes against expected future demand, and Brinkmann \emph{et al.}~\cite{brinkmann2015,brinkmann2019} study short-term
strategies and dynamic lookahead policies.
\textbf{RL.} Seo \emph{et al.}~\cite{seo2022} combine approximate dynamic programming and RL; Liang \emph{et al.}~\cite{liang2024} compare single- and dual-policy DQN
(inventory and routing); Guo \emph{et al.}~\cite{guo2024} use combinatorial RL; Pan \emph{et al.}~\cite{pan2019} use deep RL for pricing in dockless systems.
\textbf{Evolutionary methods.} Evolution strategies are a scalable alternative to policy-gradient and Q-learning methods~\cite{salimans2017}, genetic algorithms can train
deep policies~\cite{such2017}, and the field is surveyed in~\cite{bai2023}. Hybrids couple evolution with gradient-based RL, as in ERL~\cite{khadka2018} and CEM-RL~\cite{pourchot2019}, which train populations and learners jointly; our hybrid
is simpler (a frozen DQN value at the end of an evolved plan). CMA-ES~\cite{hansen2001,hansen2016} is a standard derivative-free optimizer for
low-dimensional parameters, and RHEA~\cite{perezliebana2013} evolves action sequences online against a forward model. To our knowledge these families have not been
compared with RL and Monte Carlo planning on bike repositioning under a common budget and statistical protocol; we do not reproduce the cited systems (simulators, data
and metrics differ) and our DPRL-style agent is an adaptation.

\section{Problem and Instances}
A single vehicle (capacity 20) visits $n$ stations over one operating day (06:00--22:00) in 32 intervals of 30 minutes. At each interval it chooses a destination and a
stock change at the current station ($\pm 5$, $\pm 10$ or 0 bikes), so there are $5n$ flat actions with a validity mask. Rentals and returns follow independent Poisson
processes per station and interval; the reward is the negative number of unmet rentals and returns. Every move costs one interval (there is no routing).
Rates $\lambda$ are the means of weekdays in the training weeks of Citi Bike (January--June 2024)~\cite{citibike}, with no scale factor; splits are by whole weeks.
The 4-, 8- and 12-station instances use fixed station sets; the 20- and 30-station instances add the highest-volume stations. Test uses 1\,000 scenarios sampled
from the model and, separately, 24 real weekdays from the test weeks (initial occupancy unknown, set to $50\%\pm 2$).

\section{Methods}
\textbf{Calibrated heuristics.} A greedy policy (thresholds $lo,hi$ and maximum step $d$) and a target-occupancy policy (target, thresholds, step, distance bonus). Grid search
covers both families (grids extended until the optimum is interior). The \emph{reference heuristic} of the study is the single policy with the best \emph{validation}
reward among the grid-calibrated ones and the CMA-ES runs below; the test set plays no part in the choice.
\textbf{CMA-ES calibration.} The same parameters (3 and 5) are optimized by CMA-ES~\cite{hansen2001} through a logistic box encoding with 200--203 evaluations (whole generations) on the (fixed)
validation scenarios; each run's best-on-validation point is tested once.
\textbf{DQN.} A $64$--$64$ network with action masking, replay, target network and Double DQN~\cite{mnih2015,vanhasselt2016}.
\textbf{Dual policy.} An adaptation of DPRL~\cite{liang2024}: two DQNs with shared state, one choosing the stock change (no time elapses) and one choosing the destination
in the post-inventory state; heads have 5 and $n$ outputs instead of $5n$. It uses the flat DQN's hyperparameters, untuned.
\textbf{ES (neuroevolution).} An OpenAI-style evolution strategy~\cite{salimans2017} on the \emph{same} $64$--$64$ network: antithetic sampling, centered ranks,
Adam, and the same scenarios for all members of a generation (common random numbers). The configuration chosen on validation had population \cecEsPopMax\ at every
scale, $\sigma$ from \cecEsSigMin\ to \cecEsSigMax, learning rate \cecEsLrMin--\cecEsLrMax\ and \cecEsBsMin--\cecEsBsMax\ scenarios per generation.
\textbf{Rollout.} For every valid action, the return of executing it and then following the base heuristic for $h$ decisions, averaged over $K$ futures sampled from the
\emph{model}~\cite{bertsekas1997}; $(h,K)=(3,64)$; the base heuristic is the best grid-calibrated one on validation.
\textbf{RHEA.} At each decision, a population of 4-action sequences is evolved (tournament selection, uniform crossover, per-gene mutation, elitism, shift buffer) against $K$
model futures~\cite{perezliebana2013}; horizon $L=4$ and two fixed, untuned configurations: S (population 16, 10 generations, 8 futures) and L (24, 15, 16). Their cost
per decision, in model steps, is in Table~\ref{tab:rhea}.
\textbf{RHEA with a learned value.} The fitness of a sequence becomes the mean over the $K$ futures of [rewards of the simulated steps $+\ V(s_{\mathrm{end}})$], with
$V(s)=\max_{a\ \mathrm{valid}}Q(s,a)$ from a trained, frozen DQN ($\gamma=1$, so $V$ is in the units of the reward; $V=0$ at the end of the day). The DQNs are those of the main table
(retraining with the same code, configuration and seeds reproduces their test results exactly); the search configurations S and L and the weight of the value (1) are not tuned.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
The unit of analysis is the training seed (10 per learner and scale; 5 for the 2M-step and 20/30-station runs; 3 for the longest ES budgets); evaluation episodes are not independent samples.
Hyperparameters of every learner come from a pool of 16 configurations scored on validation (2 tuning seeds, 200k steps), and the last checkpoint is reported.
All learners get the same budget in simulator steps (500k, plus 2M where stated); validation episodes during training are not counted. We report means with 95\% $t$
confidence intervals over seeds and Welch contrasts between independent runs; equivalence is claimed only with TOST~\cite{lakens2017}. Deterministic references (heuristics,
rollout, RHEA) are single values per scale, so contrasts against them are computed per seed. Deviations from the pre-registered protocol are logged in a change log before the runs, except where Section~VII says otherwise.

Fig.~\ref{fig:curves} shows why: the DQN's validation curve reaches in 500k steps a level that the ES curves do not reach in 32M. Measured in wall-clock time the gap is smaller,

\subsection{What limits RL at scale?}
The shrinking advantage of RL admits three natural explanations, which we tested one at a time. \emph{Capacity:} widening the DQN's hidden layers to 128 or 256 units
makes it worse, not better, at 12 stations (unmet demand \archBaseC\ with 64--64 against \archWideC\ and \archWiderC; Welch CIs of the reward change
[\archWideDeltaLoC; \archWideDeltaHiC] and [\archWiderDeltaLoC; \archWiderDeltaHiC]) and at 30 stations (\archBaseE\ against \archWideE\ and \archWiderE), with the other
hyperparameters untouched---a negative result that does not exclude a well-tuned wide network. \emph{Budget:} four times more steps lowers unmet demand at every scale
but leaves the DQN behind the rollout at 12, 20 and 30 stations. \emph{Action-space size:} factorizing the action into stock change and destination (dual policy) helps
at 8--20 stations but not at 30. None of the three closes the gap, and since the 20- and 30-station instances also add high-volume stations, scale and volume are
confounded. Finally, a clairvoyant planner that knows the realized demand cuts unmet demand to only \clvA, \clvB\ and \clvC\ per day at 4, 8 and 12 stations (stay:
\clvStayA, \clvStayB, \clvStayC): almost all unmet demand is avoidable with foresight, so this bound is loose and says little about the distance to the best
\emph{non-anticipative} policy.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\textbf{Where evolution helps.} The three evolutionary families behave very differently. Calibrating a handful of heuristic parameters with CMA-ES is as good as grid
search and needs a box and a step size instead of a hand-extended grid. Online evolutionary planning is a strong model-based option when its population is large enough:
RHEA-L has lower unmet demand than the 500k-step DQN at every scale. But a fixed population faces a search space that grows as $(5n)^L$, whereas the rollout, which
enumerates the first actions and relies on a calibrated base heuristic, was better in the regime we tested (12--30 stations); we did not isolate which of these two
features matters. A frozen DQN value helps the search only where the DQN is already good: at 4--12 stations it brings RHEA to the DQN's level and, with a larger search, above it,
whereas at 20 and 30 stations the search on top of a weak value is no better, or worse, than the DQN alone. Weight-space neuroevolution is by far the
most sample-hungry: at 64$\times$ the DQN's budget it at best approaches the heuristic.
\textbf{Practical reading.} With a demand model at decision time and latency of \cecMsRollA\ ms (rollout, 4 stations) to \cecMsRollE\ ms (rollout, 30 stations)
per decision in our Python implementation (shared CPU), model-based planners are at least as good as every learner from 12 stations on; where decisions must take microseconds or no model exists, a
DQN (the dual-policy variant at 8--20 stations) is the practical choice; neuroevolution of network weights is not competitive at these budgets.

\textbf{Limitations.} (i)~One city, one vehicle, and every move costs one interval (no routing time), so the results say nothing about multi-vehicle or
travel-time-aware repositioning. (ii)~The simulator and the demand model are ours and were not independently audited; learners are trained and tested on samples of the
same Poisson model, and the 24 real days are descriptive (no CIs). (iii)~The planners (rollout, RHEA) query the true demand model at decision time and no learner does,
so their advantage is not an advantage of the method class alone. (iv)~ES and DQN were tuned in a 16-configuration pool at 200k steps, the dual policy, RHEA and the value weight were not
tuned, and long-budget ES runs reuse the 200k-step configuration; a better-tuned ES or RHEA could narrow the gaps. (v)~Seeds: 10 for most 4--12 station results, but 5 at
20 and 30 stations and 3 for the longest ES budgets. (vi)~The 20- and 30-station instances add stations of higher volume, so size and volume are confounded. (vii)~Analysis
choices made after seeing results (e.g., the reference-heuristic rule) are logged with the data; the study is pre-registered only in its protocol.

\section{Conclusion}
Under one protocol, evolution was useful where the search space is small or a model is available and not where it must learn a policy from scratch from sampled returns:
CMA-ES calibration matched grid search, RHEA was competitive as an online planner, and OpenAI-ES neuroevolution of the DQN's network was not competitive even with 64
times the simulator steps. Reinforcement learning's margin over calibrated heuristics shrank from \cecRDqnA\% at 4 stations to \cecRDqnE\% at 30, and planners that query the
true demand model were at least as good as any learner from 12 stations on, at a per-decision cost of the order of $10^{\latOrderC}$ times a trained network's. A frozen DQN value made RHEA a DQN-level planner and, with a larger search, better than the DQN at 12 and 30 stations, but not better than the rollout. The main limits are a
single city, a single vehicle without routing time, and untuned dual-policy and RHEA configurations. Future work includes tuning RHEA and ES for each scale, hybrids that train the value and the search jointly, learning
the demand model instead of querying it, multi-vehicle routing and validation on operator data.
